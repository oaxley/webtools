In 2011, [xkcd 936](https://xkcd.com/936/) compared two passwords. `Tr0ub4dor&3`
has capitals, digits, a symbol and a misspelling, and looks strong. `correct horse
battery staple` is four plain words. The comic's verdict was that the second one is
much stronger *and* easier to remember.

It was right, and the reason has little to do with how a password looks. Strength
is the **number of guesses** an attacker needs. This guide shows how to count them.

## Guesses, and bits

If a password is chosen **at random**, counting is easy. Eight lowercase letters
give 26⁸, about 209 billion, possibilities. That number gets unwieldy fast, so we
write it as a power of two: 26⁸ ≈ 2³⁷·⁶, or **37.6 bits of entropy**. Each extra
bit doubles the attacker's work.

The formula is `length × log₂(alphabet size)`. Each character from the 94
printable ASCII characters adds log₂ 94 ≈ 6.55 bits:

| Random password | Possibilities | Entropy |
|---|---|---|
| 8 lowercase letters | 26⁸ | 37.6 bits |
| 8 characters, all 94 | 94⁸ | 52.4 bits |
| 12 characters, all 94 | 94¹² | 78.7 bits |
| 16 characters, all 94 | 94¹⁶ | 104.9 bits |
| 20 characters, all 94 | 94²⁰ | 131.1 bits |

Length does the heavy lifting. Going from 8 to 16 characters adds 52 bits, which
multiplies the work by 2⁵², about 4.5 million billion. Adding symbols to an
8-letter password adds 15.

## Entropy only counts for random passwords

That formula assumes every character was a uniform, independent choice. People
don't choose that way, and attackers know it. A cracker never runs through all 94¹¹
strings to find `Tr0ub4dor&3`. It tries, in order:

1. **Leaked passwords**, most common first: `123456`, `password`, `qwerty123`…
2. **Dictionary words and names**, with the usual decorations: a capital first
   letter, digits and a `!` at the end, a year.
3. **Substitutions**: `@` for `a`, `0` for `o`, `3` for `e`, `$` for `s`. Cracking
   tools like Hashcat ship rule sets that apply them to every word.
4. Keyboard runs (`qwerty`, `asdf`), sequences (`abcd`, `1234`), repeats and dates.

So `P@ssw0rd` is not 52 bits. It's `password` with two textbook substitutions and a
capital, found within the first few dozen guesses. `Tr0ub4dor&3` is `troubadour`
with one letter dropped, three substitutions, and two random characters: about 42
bits, not the 72 its length and character mix suggest.

That's why the WebTools checker works like an attacker. It looks for leaked
passwords, words, names, l33t spellings, typos, keyboard patterns, sequences and
dates, prices each piece in guesses, and keeps the cheapest way to explain the
whole password.

The result is an **upper bound**. A pattern the checker misses, such as your
dog's name or your street, makes a password look stronger than it is.

## How long would it last?

Guesses turn into time once you know the attacker's speed, which depends almost
entirely on how the site stored the password:

- **Online**, against a login form with rate limiting: about **10 guesses a second**.
- **Leaked database, slow hash** (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2id): about **10,000 a
  second** on a GPU, by design.
- **Leaked database, fast hash** (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256): about **100 billion a
  second** on a cracking rig.

On average an attacker finds the password halfway through the search. Here is what
that gives, using the same numbers as the tool:

| Password | Bits | Online | Slow hash | Fast hash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Summer2024!` | ≈ 27 | 3 months | 2 hours | instantly |
| 8 lowercase letters | 37.6 | 331 years | 4 months | 1 second |
| `Tr0ub4dor&3` | ≈ 42 | thousands of years | 7 years | 23 seconds |
| 4 random EFF words | 51.7 | millions of years | thousands of years | 5 hours |
| `correct horse battery staple` | ≈ 59 | millions of years | thousands of years | 1 month |
| 12 random characters | 78.7 | ∞ | ∞ | thousands of years |
| 6 random EFF words | 77.5 | ∞ | ∞ | thousands of years |
| 16 random characters | 104.9 | ∞ | ∞ | ∞ |

(∞: longer than the universe has existed.)

You don't get to choose how a site hashes your password, and databases leak, so
plan for the last column. **About 80 bits** is out of reach of any realistic
attacker. Anything that falls in seconds against a fast hash is only as safe as the
site's security.

## Passphrases

Random characters are strong but painful to type on a phone or a TV. A passphrase
gives the same strength from words. The trick is to let **dice, not you**, pick the
words.

The [EFF large wordlist](https://www.eff.org/dice) has 7,776 words (6⁵, five dice
rolls per word), chosen to be common, easy to spell and unambiguous. Each word drawn
at random is worth log₂ 7776 ≈ **12.9 bits**:

| Words | Entropy |
|---|---|
| 4 | 51.7 bits |
| 5 | 64.6 bits |
| 6 | 77.5 bits |
| 7 | 90.5 bits |

Six words, like `dragster-ungloved-unwound-crept-blizzard-sedative`, match a
12-character random password. That's a good size for the passwords you actually
type: a password manager's master password, disk encryption, your computer login.

Capitals and separators don't add much, because an attacker who knows the scheme
tries them too. And the words must be random. A sentence you made up, a song lyric
or a quote is a phrase someone can look up.

The xkcd passphrase shows the difference. Its four words are common ones, so the
checker prices each by how common it is rather than as 1 of 7,776, and lands on
about **59 bits**, not the 51.7 of four dice-picked words. That's still 17 bits, or
about 130,000 times more guesses, than `Tr0ub4dor&3`. But it's now one of the most
famous passwords in the world, so don't use it.

## What NIST says now

[NIST SP 800-63B](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html), the US
government's guidance on authentication, dropped many of the rules people still
associate with "secure" passwords. Revision 4 asks sites to:

- require **at least 15 characters** when the password is the only factor (8 with
  multi-factor authentication), and accept at least 64;
- accept spaces and every printable character, so passphrases and password managers
  work;
- **check new passwords against lists** of common and breached passwords;
- **not impose composition rules** ("one capital, one digit, one symbol"), which
  push people towards `Password1!`;
- **not force periodic changes**, only a change after a suspected compromise.

## Has it leaked?

A password that appears in a breach is on every attacker's list, however random it
looks. [Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/Passwords) collects hundreds of
millions of them, and you can check yours **without revealing it**, using a technique
called k-anonymity:

1. Hash the password with SHA-1. For `password`, that's
   `5BAA61E4C9B93F3F0682250B6CF8331B7EE68FD8`.
2. Send only the **first five hex digits**, `5BAA6`, to
   `api.pwnedpasswords.com/range/5BAA6`.
3. The service returns every breached hash suffix starting with that prefix, hundreds
   of them, each with a count.
4. Look for your remaining 35 digits in that list, on your own machine.

The service never sees the password or its full hash, and can't tell which of the
suffixes you were after. With the `Add-Padding: true` header, it also adds fake
entries so even the response size gives nothing away. The Check tab of the
password tool does exactly this, and only when you click the button.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Shell">
```bash
# read -s keeps the password out of the screen and your shell history.
read -rs -p 'Password: ' pw; echo
hash=$(printf '%s' "$pw" | sha1sum | cut -c1-40 | tr a-f A-F)
curl -s -H 'Add-Padding: true' "https://api.pwnedpasswords.com/range/${hash:0:5}" \
  | grep "^${hash:5}:" || echo 'Not found'
# For "password": 1E4C9B93F3F0682250B6CF8331B7EE68FD8:<count>
```
</Tab>
<Tab label="Node.js">
```js
import { createHash } from 'node:crypto'

async function pwnedCount(password) {
  const hash = createHash('sha1').update(password).digest('hex').toUpperCase()
  const res = await fetch(`https://api.pwnedpasswords.com/range/${hash.slice(0, 5)}`, {
    headers: { 'Add-Padding': 'true' },
  })
  for (const line of (await res.text()).split('\n')) {
    const [suffix, count] = line.trim().split(':')
    if (suffix === hash.slice(5)) return Number(count)
  }
  return 0
}
```
</Tab>
<Tab label="Python">
```python
import hashlib
import urllib.request

def pwned_count(password: str) -> int:
    digest = hashlib.sha1(password.encode()).hexdigest().upper()
    request = urllib.request.Request(
        f"https://api.pwnedpasswords.com/range/{digest[:5]}",
        headers={"Add-Padding": "true", "User-Agent": "pwned-check"},
    )
    with urllib.request.urlopen(request) as response:
        for line in response.read().decode().splitlines():
            suffix, count = line.split(":")
            if suffix == digest[5:]:
                return int(count)
    return 0
```

SHA-1 is [broken for collisions](/blog/hash-functions-compared), but that doesn't
matter here: it only serves as a shared index into the list.

## In practice

- **Use a password manager** and let it generate a different random password for
  every site: 16 characters or more. You never type them, so length is free.
- **Remember only a few**: the manager's master password, your computer, your
  email. Make those six-word passphrases.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication**, ideally a passkey or an authenticator app,
  on anything that matters.
- **Never reuse** a password. Reuse turns one site's breach into all of them.

## For developers

The generator is also an API, handy for test fixtures, seed data and local
`.env` files:

```bash
curl -s '@@API_BASE@@/api/v1/password?length=24&count=3'
curl -s '@@API_BASE@@/api/v1/password?mode=passphrase&words=6&separator=.'
```

Each response includes the settings and the exact `entropyBits`. AI agents can
call the same generator through the `generate_password`
[MCP tool](/blog/mcp-server-for-ai-agents), which beats a password a model makes
up: language models favour familiar-looking strings, so their "random" passwords are
much easier to guess than their length suggests. There is deliberately **no
strength-check endpoint**. A password someone chose should never be sent to a
server, ours included.

When you store passwords, a strong password is only half the job. Hash them with
**Argon2id** (or bcrypt or scrypt) through your framework's password API, never with
plain SHA-256, so that a leaked database stays in the "slow hash" column of the table
above.
